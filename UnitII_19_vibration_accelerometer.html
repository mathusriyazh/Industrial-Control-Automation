Chapter 20: Vibration & Acceleration Measurement – Electromagnetic Accelerometer Transducers

This chapter explores industrial vibration monitoring, mechanical seismic systems, electromagnetic induction sensors, piezoelectric acceleration transducers, harmonic acceleration dynamics, and natural frequency response calculations.

1. Seismic Electromagnetic & Piezoelectric Accelerometers

Industrial Hardware Image


Working Principle

An accelerometer measures structural acceleration and mechanical vibration using a spring-mass-damper seismic system enclosed within a protective housing. When subjected to external acceleration $a(t)$, the seismic mass $m$ resists displacement due to inertia.

The relative motion $z(t)$ between the seismic mass and housing is governed by Newton's Second Law and mechanical second-order system dynamics:

$$m \cdot \frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} + c \cdot \frac{dz}{dt} + k \cdot z = -m \cdot a(t)$$

In electromagnetic (moving-coil/moving-magnet) accelerometers, the relative velocity $v_r = \frac{dz}{dt}$ of the seismic element cuts through a permanent magnetic field $B$, inducing an electromotive force (EMF) $E$ according to Faraday's Law:

$$E = B \cdot L \cdot \frac{dz}{dt} = B \cdot L \cdot v_r$$

Where $B$ is magnetic flux density ($\text{Tesla}$), $L$ is coil length ($\text{m}$), and $v_r$ is relative seismic velocity. At frequencies well below the natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$, relative displacement $z$ is directly proportional to base acceleration $a$: $z \approx \frac{a}{\omega_n^2}$.

2. Natural Frequency, Damping & Harmonic Acceleration

Frequency Response & Resonance Limits

The usable linear frequency bandwidth of an accelerometer is determined by its undamped mechanical natural frequency $f_n$ ($\text{Hz}$):

$$f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

To avoid resonance distortion, accelerometers are operated below $\approx 20\%\text{--}30\%$ of their natural frequency $f_n$. Standard optimal damping ratio $\zeta \approx 0.6\text{--}0.7$ yields a flat amplitude response across the operating range.

For sinusoidal vibration displacement $x(t) = X \cdot \sin(\omega t)$, peak vibration velocity $V_{peak}$ and peak acceleration $A_{peak}$ scale quadratically with angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = \omega X \cdot \cos(\omega t) \implies V_{peak} = \omega X$$
$$a(t) = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2 X \cdot \sin(\omega t) \implies A_{peak} = \omega^2 X = (2\pi f)^2 X$$

Numerical Problems: Vibration Dynamics & Accelerometer Sensitivity

Problem 1: Peak Vibration Velocity and Acceleration

Scenario: An electric motor housing experiences steady-state harmonic vibration during operation at a frequency $f = 60\text{ Hz}$. An optical displacement sensor records a peak-to-peak vibration displacement $d_{p-p} = 0.50\text{ mm}$ ($0.0005\text{ m}$). Calculate:

  1. The peak single-amplitude displacement $X_{peak}$ in meters.
  2. The angular frequency $\omega$ in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).
  3. The peak vibration velocity $V_{peak}$ in meters per second ($\text{m/s}$).
  4. The peak acceleration $A_{peak}$ in $\text{m/s}^2$ and in engineering standard $g$-units ($1g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Calculate Peak Single-Amplitude Displacement ($X_{peak}$):

$$X_{peak} = \frac{d_{p-p}}{2} = \frac{0.50\text{ mm}}{2} = 0.25\text{ mm} = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$$

2. Calculate Angular Frequency ($\omega$):

$$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 60\text{ Hz} = 376.99\text{ rad/s}$$

3. Calculate Peak Velocity ($V_{peak}$):

$$V_{peak} = \omega \cdot X_{peak} = 376.99\text{ rad/s} \times 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.09425\text{ m/s} = 94.25\text{ mm/s}$$

4. Calculate Peak Acceleration ($A_{peak}$):

$$A_{peak} = \omega^2 \cdot X_{peak} = (376.99)^2 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 142.12 \times 0.25 = 35.53\text{ m/s}^2$$
$$A_{peak\_g} = \frac{35.53\text{ m/s}^2}{9.81\text{ m/s}^2/g} = 3.622\text{ g}$$

Problem 2: Piezoelectric Accelerometer Sensitivity & Voltage Output

Scenario: A piezoelectric vibration sensor has an acceleration sensitivity rating $S_a = 100\text{ mV/g}$. It is mounted on a gearbox bearing pedestal experiencing an RMS acceleration reading $A_{rms} = 4.5\text{ g}$. Calculate:

  1. The peak acceleration $A_{peak}$ assuming pure sinusoidal vibration.
  2. The peak-to-peak output voltage $V_{p-p}$ generated by the sensor.
  3. The RMS voltage signal output $V_{rms}$ fed to the data acquisition system.

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Calculate Peak Acceleration ($A_{peak}$):

$$A_{peak} = A_{rms} \times \sqrt{2} = 4.5\text{ g} \times 1.4142 = 6.364\text{ g}$$

2. Calculate RMS Voltage Output ($V_{rms}$):

$$V_{rms} = A_{rms} \times S_a = 4.5\text{ g} \times 100\text{ mV/g} = 450\text{ mV} = 0.450\text{ V}$$

3. Calculate Peak-to-Peak Voltage Output ($V_{p-p}$):

$$V_{p-p} = 2 \times V_{peak} = 2 \times (A_{peak} \times S_a) = 2 \times (6.364\text{ g} \times 100\text{ mV/g}) = 1272.8\text{ mV} = 1.273\text{ V}$$

Problem 3: Seismic Accelerometer Natural Frequency Calculation

Scenario: A seismic accelerometer transducer is designed with a seismic mass $m = 25\text{ grams}$ ($0.025\text{ kg}$) and a internal flexure spring constant $k = 4.0 \times 10^4\text{ N/m}$. Calculate:

  1. The undamped mechanical natural frequency $f_n$ in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$).
  2. The maximum usable flat frequency limit (assuming a safety upper limit of $20\%$ of $f_n$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Calculate Undamped Natural Frequency ($f_n$):

$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{4.0 \times 10^4\text{ N/m}}{0.025\text{ kg}}} = \sqrt{1.6 \times 10^6} = 1264.91\text{ rad/s}$$
$$f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} = \frac{1264.91\text{ rad/s}}{2\pi} = 201.32\text{ Hz}$$

2. Calculate Maximum Usable Linear Frequency Range ($f_{max}$):

$$f_{max} = 0.20 \times f_n = 0.20 \times 201.32\text{ Hz} = 40.26\text{ Hz}$$
