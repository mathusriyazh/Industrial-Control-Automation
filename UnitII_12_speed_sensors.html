Chapter 12: Rotational Speed Measurement Transducers

This chapter examines rotational velocity measurement devices used in electric drive feedback systems. Topics include solid-state magnetic potential sensing via Hall Effect Sensors, variable reluctance flux perturbation in Variable Reluctance Magnetic Speed Sensors, electromechanical generator feedback using Tachogenerators (DC & AC), and motor speed control calculations.

1. Hall Effect Speed & Position Sensor

Industrial Hardware Image


Working Principle

A Hall Effect sensor operates on the principle of Lorentz force acting on moving charge carriers inside a semiconductor strip. When a current $I$ flows through the element under a perpendicular magnetic flux density $B$, electrons are deflected toward one edge, producing a transverse Hall Voltage ($V_H$):

$$V_H = \frac{R_H \times I \times B}{t}$$

Where $R_H$ is the Hall coefficient and $t$ is element thickness. When mounted near a rotating target wheel with embedded target teeth or permanent magnets ($N$ poles), the fluctuating magnetic field creates digital pulses. The shaft rotational speed $N_{rpm}$ is calculated from pulse frequency $f$:

$$N_{rpm} = \frac{60 \times f}{P}$$

Where $P$ is the number of magnetic pole pairs or gear teeth per revolution.

2. Variable Reluctance Magnetic Speed Pickup Sensor

Industrial Hardware Image


Working Principle

A Variable Reluctance (VR) magnetic sensor is a passive transducer consisting of a permanent magnet wrapped with a coil wire around a soft iron pole piece. When a ferrous gear tooth passes close to the sensor tip, the air gap magnetic reluctance decreases, increasing magnetic flux $\Phi$.

According to Faraday’s Law of Electromagnetic Induction, the changing flux induces an AC sine-wave voltage $e(t)$ across the coil output:

$$e(t) = -N_{turns} \times \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

The output signal amplitude and frequency both increase proportionally with target rotation speed $\omega$. VR sensors require no external DC power source.

3. Tachogenerator Transducer (DC & AC)

Industrial Hardware Image


Working Principle

A Tachogenerator (Tacho-generator) is a small electrical generator mechanically coupled to a rotating machine shaft. It converts mechanical rotational velocity directly into an analog voltage output.

In a DC Tachogenerator, a permanent magnet stator induces a DC voltage in the rotating armature. The output voltage $V_g$ is directly proportional to shaft speed $\omega$ ($N_{rpm}$):

$$V_g = K_t \times \omega = K_t \times \left( \frac{2\pi N_{rpm}}{60} \right)$$

Where $K_t$ is the tachometer constant (expressed in $\text{V/RPM}$ or $\text{V/rad/s}$). The polarity of $V_g$ indicates the direction of rotation.

Comparative Analysis: Speed Sensors

Sensor Type Output Signal Format Zero-Speed Detection? Advantages Disadvantages
Hall Effect Sensor Digital Square Wave pulses ($0-5\text{V}$ or open collector). Yes (Can detect static zero speed). Compact, robust, integrated signal conditioning, reliable digital noise immunity. Requires active external DC power supply; limited operating temperature compared to VR.
Variable Reluctance (VR) Sensor Analog AC Sine wave (amplitude & frequency increase with speed). No (Fails at near-zero speeds due to low induced voltage). Passive (no power needed), extremely rugged, operates at very high temperatures ($> 200^\circ\text{C}$). Signal drops to zero at low speeds; requires sensitive threshold detection circuitry.
DC Tachogenerator Continuous analog DC voltage proportional to speed; polarity indicates direction. Yes (Linear continuous voltage output near zero). Direct velocity feedback for analog closed-loop motor drives without ADC decoding. Commutator brush wear and electrical spark noise; higher weight and cost.

Numerical Problems: Speed Measurement & Motor Control

Problem 1: Hall Effect Sensor Interfacing with BLDC Motor

Scenario: A 4-pole pair ($P = 4$) Brushless DC (BLDC) motor has three internal Hall Effect sensors positioned $120^\circ$ apart. A frequency counter attached to one Hall sensor measures a pulse frequency $f = 240\text{ Hz}$. Calculate:

  1. The motor rotational speed in revolutions per minute ($N_{rpm}$).
  2. The mechanical angular velocity ($\omega$) in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Rotational Speed ($N_{rpm}$):

$$N_{rpm} = \frac{60 \times f}{P} = \frac{60 \times 240\text{ Hz}}{4} = 3600\text{ RPM}$$

2. Angular Velocity ($\omega$):

$$\omega = \frac{2\pi \times N_{rpm}}{60} = \frac{2\pi \times 3600}{60} = 120\pi = 376.99\text{ rad/s}$$

Problem 2: Variable Reluctance Sensor on Turbine Shaft Gear

Scenario: A magnetic speed pickup sensor monitors a 60-tooth steel gear ($P = 60\text{ teeth}$) mounted on an industrial steam turbine drive shaft. An electronic digital tachometer counts the sensor pulses over a gate time window of $t_{gate} = 0.5\text{ seconds}$ and counts $N_{pulses} = 1800\text{ pulses}$. Calculate:

  1. The pulse frequency $f$ generated by the pickup sensor.
  2. The shaft speed in RPM ($N_{rpm}$).
  3. The pulse frequency if the turbine shaft speed increases to $N_{new} = 4500\text{ RPM}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Signal Frequency ($f$):

$$f = \frac{N_{pulses}}{t_{gate}} = \frac{1800}{0.5\text{ s}} = 3600\text{ Hz}$$

2. Shaft Speed ($N_{rpm}$):

$$N_{rpm} = \frac{60 \times f}{P} = \frac{60 \times 3600\text{ Hz}}{60\text{ teeth}} = 3600\text{ RPM}$$

3. Frequency at $4500\text{ RPM}$ ($f_{new}$):

$$f_{new} = \frac{N_{new} \times P}{60} = \frac{4500 \times 60}{60} = 4500\text{ Hz}$$

Problem 3: DC Tachogenerator Feedback for PMDC Motor Drive

Scenario: A PMDC motor is coupled to a DC tachogenerator with a rated sensitivity constant $K_t = 0.05\text{ V/RPM}$ ($50\text{ mV/RPM}$). The tachogenerator output is loaded by an operational amplifier circuit with input impedance $R_L = 50\text{ k}\Omega$. The internal armature resistance of the tachogenerator is $R_g = 500\ \Omega$. When the motor runs at $N = 1800\text{ RPM}$, calculate:

  1. The ideal open-circuit tachogenerator output voltage ($V_{open}$).
  2. The actual loaded output voltage ($V_{loaded}$) across $R_L$.
  3. The percentage measurement error caused by loading.

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Ideal Open-Circuit Voltage ($V_{open}$):

$$V_{open} = K_t \times N_{rpm} = 0.05\text{ V/RPM} \times 1800\text{ RPM} = 90.00\text{ V}$$

2. Loaded Output Voltage ($V_{loaded}$): Applying voltage divider between $R_g$ and $R_L$:

$$V_{loaded} = V_{open} \times \left( \frac{R_L}{R_g + R_L} \right) = 90.00 \times \left( \frac{50000}{500 + 50000} \right) = 90.00 \times \left( \frac{50000}{50500} \right) = 89.109\text{ V}$$

3. Percentage Loading Error:

$$\text{Error (\%)} = \frac{V_{loaded} - V_{open}}{V_{open}} \times 100 = \frac{89.109 - 90.00}{90.00} \times 100 = -0.99\%$$
